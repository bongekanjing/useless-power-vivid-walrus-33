# Drafts — "One Line, Zero Use"

Fifteen powers, written here first. The rule I'm testing each one against: the
benefit has to be stated plainly, and the uselessness has to arrive as a
consequence in the last few words, not as an explanation.

1. **I can reheat any cup of coffee with a single thought, but only to exactly
   room temperature.**
   Strong — the power is a no-op, which is the purest uselessness. But it's a
   paradox gag more than a scene; you smile, you don't laugh.

2. **I can instantly identify any spice in a dish, and I am never once asked to.**
   Tight and deadpan, and "never once asked to" is a good closing turn. Kills on
   picturability: nothing happens in the reader's head.

3. **I can unlock any door with my mind, but only doors that were already unlocked.**
   Clean shape, lands hard, but this is the stock useless-power joke — the shape
   has been done to death.

4. **I can fold a fitted sheet perfectly in eleven seconds, and my reward is being
   asked to fold the fitted sheet.**
   Domestic and specific, but the drawback is a chore rather than a consequence.
   No surprise, just homework.

5. **I can always find a parking space within a block, but only when I'm walking there.**
   Real laugh, real picture. Fails on vagueness: "walking there" doesn't show me
   anything, and the power looks like luck rather than a power.

6. **I can make any printer work by touching it, but only the printer in the office
   I was fired from.**
   Funny and specific, but it needs a whole backstory to land. Too much freight
   for one line.

7. **I can talk to plants and they talk back, but only to discuss my hairline.**
   Gets a laugh. The drawback is arbitrary rather than useless, though — that's
   not a useless power, that's an insulting one.

8. **I can pause time, but only for as long as it takes to realise I've paused time.**
   Neat paradox, but it's a thought experiment, not a picture. And "pause time"
   is the least fresh power on the board.

9. **I can hear the sea in any seashell, including the ones that are actually just
   croissants.**
   Surreal and charming, but it drifts: the joke is about croissants, not about
   the power's uselessness.

10. **I can reheat any cup of coffee by staring at it, but only to exactly room
    temperature.**
    Ruled out: "by staring at it" echoes the sponsor's toast example too closely.

11. **I can win any argument in perfect detail, in my head, on the drive home.**
    Very funny and universal, but it's a familiar phrase ("l'esprit de
    l'escalier") wearing a cape. Not fresh.

12. **I can hold my breath for an hour, but only in a bathtub I've just cleaned.**
    Good domestic detail, weak consequence — the reader has to work out why
    that's a loss.

13. **I can make any baby stop crying, but only by crying louder, and then they
    sleep and I don't.**
    Two reversals, so neither one lands. Explaining the trade-off out loud.

14. **I can spot a counterfeit bill from across the room, but I only ever get
    handed the real ones.**
    Solid, clean, but it's a small power in a bank, and banks are not funny.

15. **I can hold any elevator door open forever, but whoever I'm holding it for is
    already four floors up the stairs.**
    Winner.

## Why 15 wins

It's a superpower nobody claims: not flight, not invisibility, but the
door-holding reflex of a polite person, pushed to an absurd literal extreme
(*forever*). The benefit is granted instantly and completely — the door never
closes — and then the line spends its second half on a single image: the person
you are waiting for has committed to the stairs and is already above you.

The reversal lands on the last three words, *up the stairs*, and it lands as a
consequence rather than a label. Nobody in the line explains that this is
useless; the reader does the arithmetic. "Four floors" is the load-bearing
detail — without a number it's just "they took the stairs," with it the reader
sees the whole climb and sees you standing there, arm out, for nobody.

Two things I beat out of it while reading aloud:

- *indefinitely* → *forever*. Same meaning, two syllables shorter, and it makes
  the pledge sound more solemn, which makes standing there funnier.
- *the person I'm holding it for* → *whoever it's for*. The first version hit a
  for/is collision that stumbled when read aloud, and it also assumed you knew
  who you were waiting on. Cutting it to *whoever it's for* is eight characters
  shorter and funnier: you don't even know, and the door is still open.

Final character count is under 200 with room to spare, and there is no wind-up:
the power is named in the first eight words.
